# Concept check

Test a concept the way Gerring proposes: as a set of trade-offs among criteria, judged against the alternatives the writer could have chosen, not against an absolute standard. Add Sartori's test for conceptual stretching.

Sources:
- John Gerring, "What Makes a Concept Good? A Criterial Framework for Understanding Concept Formation in the Social Sciences," *Polity* 31.3 (1999): 357–393.
- John Gerring, *Social Science Methodology: A Unified Framework*, 2nd ed. (Cambridge University Press, 2012), ch. 5, "Concepts," 107–140.
- Giovanni Sartori, "Concept Misformation in Comparative Politics," *American Political Science Review* 64.4 (1970): 1033–1053.

## Modes

1. **Audit**: the user's own concept, in their draft. Map, diagnose, flag, and propose moves with their costs. Do not rewrite the text.
2. **Read**: a concept in someone else's text. Map it and say what the concept buys the argument and what it costs. No verdict unless asked.
3. **Design**: a concept still being formed. Run Gerring's sequence (survey near-synonyms, classify attributes, choose a definition strategy) and lay out the options with their trade-offs.

Infer the mode from the input; state the assumption. Check one to three concepts per run, the ones the argument depends on. If the user does not name them, pick the coined terms and any borrowed term the text redefines or leans on, and say which ones you picked.

## Core principles

- **Trade-offs, not rules.** Every criterion has a cost elsewhere. Gerring: "although particular criterial demands are often ignored, they are ignored at a cost." Say which criteria the writer has favoured and what that costs. Do not demand that all criteria be maximised.
- **Relative standard.** Judge the concept against named alternatives: the existing term it replaces, a rival definition, the writer's own other uses. "Weak differentiation" means nothing until you say: weaker than what?
- **Four elements move together**: term, attributes (intension), recognition cues or indicators, and instances (extension). A change to one changes the others.
- **No scores.** Use *gain / cost / neutral / n.a.* against each alternative, with evidence quoted from the text.

## Criteria

Gerring's 1999 and 2012 lists differ. This skill uses both, merged; the source of each criterion is given in brackets.

- **Familiarity**: how far the term and its definition fit established usage, in everyday language and in the relevant specialist community. Dropping a core feature of the usual meaning costs more than dropping a peripheral one. Neologism is the strongest violation and needs a stated reason why no existing term will do. [1999 familiarity; 2012 "resonance"]
- **Click**: whether the term sticks, through metaphor, rhythm, brevity or fit with a set of sibling terms. Real but dangerous: catchy labels tempt writers away from familiarity. [1999 resonance; dropped in 2012]
- **Parsimony**: whether the term is short and the defining attributes few. Parsimony of the term and of the definition can pull apart. [1999]
- **Domain**: which language communities the concept must work in (one subfield, a discipline, lay readers, a second language) and which empirical terrain it covers. A definition can be adequate in one domain and fail in another. [2012]
- **Consistency**: whether the concept means the same thing everywhere it is used in the work. [2012]
- **Coherence and depth**: whether the defining attributes belong together (logically or functionally) rather than co-occurring by accident, and how many further properties the instances share. A core principle that subsumes the attributes gives the most coherence. A definition by what a thing is *not* can mark a boundary but adds no depth. [1999 coherence + depth; 2012 "fecundity"]
- **Differentiation**: how clearly the concept is bounded against its neighbours (near-synonyms, antonyms, the broader and narrower terms), and against what contrast space. It is bounded in meaning and, where relevant, in time and place. [1999; 2012]
- **Field utility**: what the concept does to its neighbours. A good concept unsettles its semantic field as little as possible. It does not take referents or attributes from adjacent terms, and it does not relabel the same parts under a new name. [1999; folded into differentiation in 2012]
- **Theoretical utility**: what work the concept does in the argument. What would be lost if an existing term replaced it? For causal claims: does it separate cause from effect, avoiding circularity? [1999 theoretical utility; 2012 "causal utility"]
- **Recognition**: how the reader can tell an instance when they see one. Gerring treats operationalisation as part of differentiation: "If you don't know it when you see it, then you can't tell it (the concept) from other things." For interpretive concepts, read this as: can the reader identify instances from the text's own criteria? [2012 operationalisation]

## Sartori's tests

- **Ladder of abstraction.** A concept is made more general by **dropping** attributes: the range of cases grows, and the attributes that remain stay precise. It is made more specific by **adding** attributes. Place the concept as high, medium or low level, and check that it is used at the level it is defined for.
- **Conceptual stretching.** The concept is extended to more cases without dropping attributes, and the attributes are left vague instead. Sartori: "the denotation is extended by obfuscating the connotation." The result is "a mere generality," not a general concept. The test: when the text applies the concept to a new case, does every defining attribute still hold? If not, was an attribute openly dropped (legitimate climbing), or silently loosened (stretching)?
- **Exceptions.** This inverse relation between attributes and range holds when attributes are necessary conditions or matters of degree. When each attribute is sufficient on its own, adding attributes widens the range of cases (Gerring 2012, fig. 5.1). Theoretical terms defined by their place in a theory (Sartori's examples: feedback, homeostasis) are not reached by climbing the ladder. Do not fault them by ladder rules; mark the test n.a.

## Definition strategies

Identify which strategy the text uses (Gerring 2012, table 5.2), and check that its use matches:

- **Minimal**: necessary (and jointly sufficient) conditions. This sets a threshold, so cases are either in or out. Crisp borders; costs familiarity, because the stripped-down meaning sounds thin.
- **Maximal (ideal type)**: all non-idiosyncratic attributes, in the purest form. Cases resemble it by degree, and no real case needs to fit it fully.
- **Cumulative**: attributes ranked by how central they are, which gives an ordinal scale.

A common mismatch is an ideal type used to sort cases in or out, or a minimal definition loaded with the claims of a maximal one.

## Procedure

1. **Concept card.** For each concept, record: the term; each definition stated in the text, quoted with its location; the attributes; the recognition cues; the instances the text names; the strategy; the level on the ladder; and the domain the text seems to intend. If there is no explicit definition, say so. Reconstruct the working definition from the uses, and label it as reconstructed.
2. **Uses pass.** List every significant use with location, a short verbatim anchor, and the sense in play. Mark drift, and any extension to new cases.
3. **Contrast space.** List the neighbours: first those the text itself names, then those the field would expect. Label which come from the text and which come from your own knowledge. Treat your own claims about "established usage" as checkable, and say what would check them (a dictionary, a field handbook, the canonical source).
4. **Alternatives.** Choose two or three alternatives to judge against: the nearest existing term, a rival definition, and the minimal or maximal version of the writer's own concept. In Design mode, also run Gerring's survey: near-synonyms and their attributes, reduced to one table of attributes.
5. **Trade-off table.** For each criterion, give gain / cost / neutral / n.a. against each alternative, with a one-line reason tied to the text.
6. **Diagnosis.** Which criteria the writer favours, at whose expense, and whether the price is worth what the argument gets. Note any move that improves one criterion at no cost to the others (Gerring's "Pareto" move). Make that first, because every move after it costs something.
7. **Flags**, each with location and whether it touches the thesis or is local.
8. **Moves** (Audit, Design): rename; add or drop an attribute; narrow the domain; state the contrast; change strategy; split the concept in two. Give each with the criterion it serves and the criterion it costs.

## Flags

- **F1 Stretching.** The concept is applied to a case its attributes do not fit, with no attribute openly dropped. [Sartori]
- **F2 Drift.** The meaning shifts between uses in the same work. [consistency]
- **F3 Unargued neologism.** A new term, or a sharp redefinition, with no account of why existing terms fail. Robinson's rule, quoted by Gerring: "stipulate as little as possible." [familiarity]
- **F4 Missing contrast.** Neighbours go unaddressed: the text never says what the concept is not, or where it stops. [differentiation]
- **F5 Relabelling.** A new name for the same set of parts, or attributes taken from a neighbouring term, which leaves that term empty. [field utility]
- **F6 Incoherent bundle.** The defining attributes bear no relation to one another, or contradict one another (Gerring's "curly head-of-state"). [coherence]
- **F7 Shallow or residual.** The concept is defined mainly by what it is not, or its instances share little beyond the definition. [depth]
- **F8 Strategy mismatch.** An ideal type is used to sort cases in or out, or a minimal definition is made to carry maximal claims. [Gerring 2012, table 5.2]
- **F9 Idle concept.** Replacing it with the nearest existing term changes nothing in the argument. [theoretical utility]
- **F10 Unrecognisable.** The text gives no means of telling an instance from a non-instance. [recognition]
- **F11 Ambiguous.** The text supports two readings of the concept and nothing in it decides between them. Give both and say what hangs on each. Do not resolve it by guessing intent.

## Concepts in two languages

Familiarity and click are judged per language community. When a concept travels between languages (a Russian term and its English rendering, say), check each domain separately. Note where the translation shifts the attributes, the neighbours or the register, because the concept may pass in one language and fail in the other.

## Output

Lead with the diagnosis, then the flags, then the concept card, the uses table and the trade-off table. Then the moves (Audit, Design). No closing summary. For a long text, put the uses table in a file and give the rest in the reply.

## Don't

- Score concepts or rank them on a scale.
- Treat a criterion as a rule the concept must satisfy.
- Fault a theoretical term for failing ladder or recognition tests designed for empirical concepts.
- Present your own sense of "established usage" as fact without marking it.
- Rewrite the text. Propose moves; edit only on explicit request.
- Force one reading of an ambiguous concept.
